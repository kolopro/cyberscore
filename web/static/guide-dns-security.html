DNS security: CAA records, DNSSEC and common misconfigurations

The DNS is the first step in every web connection. Getting it wrong opens the door to certificate fraud, spoofing and traffic hijacking.

In short: DNS translates your domain name into an IP address. Without DNS security controls, attackers can forge DNS answers or trick certificate authorities into issuing fake certificates for your domain. Add a CAA record to restrict who can issue certs, and consider DNSSEC to cryptographically sign your DNS data.

What DNS actually does

When someone types example.com into a browser, their device asks a chain of DNS resolvers "what is the IP address for this domain?" The resolver works up through root servers, TLD servers and finally your authoritative name server — all in a fraction of a second — and returns the answer. That answer is then used for every connection: loading the website, sending email, verifying certificates.

DNS was designed in 1983, before internet security was a concern, and it shows. Without security extensions, there is no built-in way to verify that an answer has not been tampered with on its way back to the requester.

CAA records: controlling who can issue certificates for your domain

A Certification Authority Authorization (CAA) record is a DNS record that tells every certificate authority (CA) in the world whether it is permitted to issue a TLS certificate for your domain. CAs are required by industry rules to check CAA records before issuing a certificate.

Without a CAA record, any of the hundreds of trusted CAs can issue a certificate for your domain — even if you have only ever used Let's Encrypt. If a CA is compromised or makes an error, your domain could receive a fraudulent certificate that browsers would trust. A CAA record limits that blast radius.

Example CAA records

# Allow only Let's Encrypt to issue standard certificates
example.com. CAA 0 issue "letsencrypt.org"

# Allow only Let's Encrypt to issue wildcard certificates
example.com. CAA 0 issuewild "letsencrypt.org"

# Send violation reports here
example.com. CAA 0 iodef "mailto:security@example.com"

To add these, go to your domain's DNS management panel (wherever you registered the domain or manage DNS — Cloudflare, Route53, your registrar) and add CAA records. If you use multiple CAs, add one issue record per CA.

Common CA hostnames for CAA records: letsencrypt.org (Let's Encrypt), digicert.com, sectigo.com, amazon.com (AWS ACM).

DNSSEC: cryptographic signatures for DNS answers

DNSSEC (DNS Security Extensions) adds a layer of cryptographic signatures to DNS records. When DNSSEC is enabled, each DNS answer comes with a signature that resolvers can verify using a chain of public keys published in DNS itself. A tampered or forged answer will have an invalid signature and be rejected.

DNSSEC protects against DNS cache poisoning — a class of attack where a malicious resolver returns a forged IP address, redirecting users to an attacker's server. The attack was dramatically demonstrated by security researcher Dan Kaminsky in 2008 and remains a theoretical threat on networks with weak resolver software.

DNSSEC in practice

Enabling DNSSEC is usually done in two places:

  1. Your authoritative DNS provider generates and publishes the zone-signing keys and signatures. Most managed DNS providers (Cloudflare, Route53, Google Cloud DNS) can do this with one click.
  2. Your domain registrar publishes a Delegation Signer (DS) record at the parent zone (the TLD), creating the chain of trust. This step requires coordination between your DNS provider and your registrar — most registrars support it, though the UI varies.
Cloudflare makes DNSSEC especially simple: enable it in the DNS tab and it handles key generation, signing and DS record publishing automatically via your registrar's EPP protocol. For Route53, enable signing in the hosted zone settings and copy the DS record to your registrar.

Common DNS misconfigurations to avoid

Subdomain takeover

If a DNS record points to a service (a CDN, a cloud platform, a SaaS tool) that you have since deactivated, an attacker can claim that service's endpoint and serve content from your subdomain. Regularly audit CNAME and A records pointing to third-party services and remove them when the service is discontinued.

Open DNS resolvers

Running your own DNS resolver and leaving it open to arbitrary queries from the internet makes it a tool for DNS amplification DDoS attacks. Restrict your resolver to serve only your own hosts, or use a managed resolver like Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1 instead of running your own.

Zone transfer exposure

DNS zone transfers (AXFR) let secondary name servers copy your full zone file. If AXFR is open to all IPs, anyone can retrieve your complete internal DNS map — a useful reconnaissance tool for attackers. Restrict zone transfers to known secondary IP addresses only.

Wildcard DNS abuse

A wildcard record like *.example.com → 1.2.3.4 is convenient, but it can mask dangling subdomain records and complicate your CAA policy. Use wildcards deliberately, and pair them with issuewild CAA records to control wildcard certificate issuance separately from standard certificate issuance.
