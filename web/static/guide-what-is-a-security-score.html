What is a website security score & how is it calculated?

A single letter grade summarises complex technical checks. Here's exactly what goes into it and how to move yours up.

In short: A security score converts the results of multiple technical checks — HTTPS, security headers, DNS configuration, email authentication — into a 0–100 number and an A–F grade. It is a quick health indicator, not a certification. Fixing the highest-severity findings raises it fastest.

Why does a score exist at all?

A website's security posture spans dozens of settings across TLS configuration, HTTP response headers, DNS records, email authentication and more. Reading every raw result takes expertise and time. A security score collapses all of that into one number so a site owner, developer or executive can see at a glance whether the basics are covered — and immediately know what priority to focus on first.

Think of it like a car's MOT: a pass tells you the vehicle meets minimum road-safety standards; a fail lists the specific items that need attention. The score doesn't replace detailed review, but it gives you a reliable starting point.

What CyberScore checks

Every scan runs five independent modules and combines their findings into one score:

  • HTTPS & TLS — is HTTPS available? Does HTTP redirect to HTTPS? What protocol versions and cipher suites does the server offer? Is the certificate valid, strong and not expiring soon?
  • HTTP security headers — are HSTS, Content-Security-Policy, X-Frame-Options, X-Content-Type-Options, Referrer-Policy and Permissions-Policy present in responses?
  • DNS configuration — are there CAA records limiting which CAs can issue certificates for the domain? Are name-server setups consistent?
  • Email authentication — do SPF, DKIM and DMARC records exist and are they configured to actually reject spoofed mail (not just monitor it)?
  • Technology detection — is the site advertising its software version in headers, giving attackers a shortcut to known vulnerabilities?

How the number is calculated

Every finding is classified by severity:

  • HIGH — a serious gap that meaningfully increases risk (e.g. HTTPS not available, DMARC missing with no p=reject policy). Each deducts 20 points.
  • MEDIUM — a significant but not critical gap (e.g. missing HSTS, CSP absent). Each deducts 10 points.
  • LOW — a best-practice miss that marginally increases risk (e.g. missing Referrer-Policy). Each deducts 3 points, up to a maximum of 20 total from low findings.

The score starts at 100 and findings are subtracted, never going below 0. This means ten low-severity findings cannot drag a score lower than eighty if no higher-severity issues exist — the system rewards getting the important things right.

What the score doesn't tell you

A high score means the common, automatable controls are in place. It does not mean the site's application logic is free of SQL injection, that access controls are correct, or that the content itself is safe. Those require manual testing or a full penetration test. The score is best understood as a baseline hygiene check — necessary, but not sufficient on its own.

A score of 100 does not mean "unhackable." It means every passively verifiable best practice has been applied. Active vulnerabilities (XSS, IDOR, broken auth) are outside the scope of passive scanning.

How to improve your score quickly

  1. Fix HIGH findings first. Each one costs 20 points. Enabling HTTPS and adding a strict DMARC policy are common quick wins that together recover 40 points.
  2. Add missing security headers. HSTS, CSP and X-Frame-Options are three MEDIUM findings — 30 points — and they can usually be set in a few lines of server config or a CDN rule.
  3. Revisit LOW findings once the highs and mediums are cleared. Referrer-Policy and Permissions-Policy are one-line additions that push a B toward an A.

CyberScore's Remediation tab lists every finding in priority order with copy-ready fixes specific to your result. Start there after a scan.

How often should you check your score?

A good rule of thumb is after every major infrastructure change (new server, CDN migration, certificate renewal) and at least quarterly otherwise. Security configurations drift: a header added in one deployment can be inadvertently dropped in the next, or a certificate can expire if auto-renewal silently fails. Regular checks catch these regressions early.
